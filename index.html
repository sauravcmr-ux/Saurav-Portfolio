One story. Six ways to tell it.

I write about families and the bills they didn't see coming — as research, as the pages that sell a platform, as the words inside it, as video scripts, and as screenplays in Hindi, Bhojpuri and English. Same preoccupation every time. Below is one of them, told six ways.

The brief
A family is handed a bill at the exact moment it has the least room to argue about it.

What I write

Six formats, one habit: find the single true thing the piece is about, then cut everything that isn't holding it up.

Research & features

Data-led reports and explainers. I'm at my best when a subject looks dull from the outside — a fee table, an admission rule — and turns out to have a mechanism inside it that changes what a reader does next.

Analysis, methodology note, full draft, charts specced.

Writing about businesses

Company and school profiles, founder stories, case studies, ghostwritten posts. The work is mostly getting people to say the interesting version of what they do, then holding them to it on the page.

Interview, draft, one round of stakeholder wrangling.

Site & marketing copy

Homepages, category and listing pages, short descriptions, email. Most sites fail because nobody decided what the business is actually claiming. I start there, then write the shortest version that still earns the click.

Messaging notes, page-by-page drafts, two revision rounds.

Product & UI copy

Buttons, empty states, errors, onboarding, form labels. The reader here is mid-task and slightly anxious — a parent halfway through an application — so every word has to earn its place and none of them get to be clever.

Flow-by-flow copy, a voice guide, terminology kept consistent.

Video scripts

Shorts built around a single turn, and long-form scripts with an argument underneath. Written the way someone would say it out loud, not the way it reads on paper.

Hook variants, beat outline, full script, on-screen text.

Screenplays & short fiction

Short scripts and prose in Hindi, Bhojpuri and English — mostly two-handers built for a small cast and one location, and occasionally farce. Writing dialogue in a language people actually argue in is what keeps the rest of the work honest.

Original shorts, commissions, dialogue passes, translation between the three.

Research feature The Ezyschooling Fee IndexFee study on private schools in India 2026

Tuition rises land in a renewal notice, so a family sees them coming. A deposit increase appears once, unannounced, attached to an offer of admission — at the exact moment a family has the least room to compare or negotiate.

Built from 119,629 school-class-session records across 6,879 schools and 42 cities. Wrote the methodology, the analysis and the report: fee composition, city and board comparisons, revision frequency, and a fourteen-year total cost of ₹14.6 lakh per child.

Screenplays & short fiction

Nine pieces in three languages. They keep circling one thing — somebody is handed a bill they can't refuse — which turns out to work as tragedy and, when I let it, as farce.

Screenplay · Bhojpuri Terah DinThirteen days of funeral rites, and what grief is billed at Short

Babuji chal gahilan magar karza chhod gahilan. Ab dekha, humnike kekar terhavi manawake — garibi ka, ya aapan?
Father's gone but he left the debt. So whose thirteenth day are we observing — our poverty's, or his?

An old man dies at dawn and the family has thirteen days to bury him properly. The priest's list grows, the land dispute surfaces, and a six-year-old knocks over the ashes. Written in Bhojpuri.

Short fiction · English The Last ShiftSeven men sent to seal a radioactive breach Prose

They weren't friends. Just men. Men with no wives, no kids. No one waiting. Just names on a government list: Single. No dependents.

Told in fourteen numbered days, one name crossed off the board at a time. The men were chosen for the job because nobody would come asking after them, and the story never lets you forget that was the selection criterion.

Screenplay · Hindi Mrit Aur MrityuA man meets Death, who turns out to know less than he does Short

Mujhe kisi cheez ka yakeen nahi. Mere paas khud ko kisi aur se tulna karne ke liye kabhi kuch raha hi nahi.

A two-hander that runs on one idea: Death has no answers, no scales, possibly no hands, and has never once been asked what it wants.

Screenplay · Hindi Abhi Bhi Yahin HoonA father talks into the dark, and one night it answers Short

Yeh maafi nahi hai. Main woh maaf nahi kar sakta jo mujhe yaad hi nahi ki aapne mere saath kiya.

After a crash, a father needs to be absolved and his son cannot do it — not out of anger, but because the memory that would make absolution possible isn't there.

Screenplay · Hindi LifafaTwo brothers and their father's last letter Short

Unhone apni aakhri likhi hui cheez ka bharosa mujh par kiya. Tum par nahi.

A sealed envelope on a desk between two men, neither of whom will let go of it. The argument is never really about the letter.

Screenplay · English The CertificateA builder, an engineer, and two per cent Short

"Two percent of eighty lakh. That's my entire margin, sir. You're asking for my profit." — "I'm asking for the file to move. What you call it is your business."

Set in a site office at a half-built government school. Three to four minutes, two actors, one table.

Screenplay · Hindi SawaalA father, a minister, and a leaked exam paper Short

Aapne kuch karne ko nahi kaha. Bas kuch rokne ko bhi nahi kaha.

No cameras, no press conference. A man who lost a son to an institution's convenience asks the one question nobody made the institution answer.

Comedy · English Broke KingdomA bankrupt monarchy, in four acts Short

"Fine. I abdicate — but only because I'm tired and I miss butter."

The king threw the kingdom's grain into fountains for his birthday, the princess gambled the last tiara on a squirrel race, and the revolutionary at the gate has a legitimate grievance and an illegitimate birth. Farce, but the bread riots are real.

Comedy · English Planned & PreparedA man empties a pharmacy before anyone knows why Short

"For what — an alien attack?" — "Aliens don't replicate exponentially."

Ten minutes of deadpan between a hypochondriac in an ill-fitting PPE suit and a bored pharmacy clerk. The joke runs the whole script; the muted television under the counter finishes it.

Site and product copy

Most of this work is live inside a product rather than published under a byline — listing pages, comparison flows, forms and the text that walks a parent through an application. I can walk through specific pages and the thinking behind them on a call.

About

I'm a senior content writer with a business management background and about five years of writing behind me, three and a half of them in edtech.

Most of that time has been spent on one audience: parents trying to make an expensive, irreversible decision about a child's schooling, usually with incomplete information and a deadline. That's shaped how I write everything else. If a sentence doesn't help someone decide or do something, it goes.

The fiction is the same preoccupation in another form. A priest itemising funeral rites for a family that can't afford them is the fee report with the numbers taken out. A bankrupt king throwing grain into fountains is the same thing played for laughs. I didn't plan either; I noticed it after the fact.

The range is the useful part. One brief can become a report, a landing page, the microcopy inside the product, or a ninety-second script — and I'd rather one person hold all of them than have four people argue about tone.
